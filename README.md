# Solar Generators, Add-on Batteries, and Panels

## Generator

A generator is a single box that contains three major components.

### Battery

The battery is a chemical engine that stores energy as electric charge. The capacity of the
battery is usually given in kWh, kilowatt hours. Generally, the battery cannot (or should not)
be completely drained, so the *usable* energy is often 95% of the nominal capacity.

To get a general range of battery sizes in kWh, a current production model mobile phone
is often around 0.015 kWh, and an EV battery is somewhere around 80 kWh. IOW, a large range.

#### Add-on Batteries

Many larger units have the ability to add additional batteries without having
to buy the charging circuits and inverters an additional time. This generally saves
around 25% to 50% on the cost per kWh. However, you generally benefit from having
one generator-system per floor of your house -- otherwise, you wind up with extension
cords in the stairwell. 

#### Suggestion

Models in the 3kWh to 5kWh range with expansion capability are competitively 
priced. If you do not plan on running a lot of high wattage devices simultaneously,
there are 1kWh models that have expansion capability, and that can save money, too.

Regardless, I would get a couple of small units in the 0.3 to 0.5 kWh range.
They are convenient for fans, CPAP machines, and other small devices.

### Inverter

This component converts the battery's stored energy into an 120 VAC power
source. Some models also offer 240 VAC. If you plan on running anything
with a motor in it (even a fan), be sure to get a generator with sine
wave output.

The inverter + battery have two important specs in combination: maximum
sustained power and maximum surge power. With current battery technology,
the maximum sustained output in watts is often near the capacity in kWh,
meaning that the battery can be drained in an hour if it is at maximum
output. The surge power is often around twice the sustained output. This
is one way that a generator differs from an EV's battery --- an EV
battery can often produce a staggering amount of power, but only for
ten to fifteen seconds.

### Charging cicuits

The first two items are relatively simple, and the specs are not complex. But, ...

There are usually multiple ways to charge the battery, with almost all
generators including 120 VAC so that you can plug into the wall to charge,
and DC inputs for solar panels.

**There are a wide variety of limits of DC current and DC voltage among
very similar units.** Three numbers matter more than the others:

**Maximum input voltage** Solar panels differ in their output voltage,
and they are generally lumped into classes by the vendors. 12V, 18V,
24V, and 32V are common sizes.  The generator's maximum input voltage
must exceed the output voltage of whatever type of panels you use.

**Maximum input current** Large amounts of current require large
wires, and components in the assembly that are capable of handing the
current. The max input current is often around 20A.

**Maximum input power** Sadly, multiplying volts and amps may not give
you this number.  For example, you might find that a generator rated to
accept 24V and 20A might be limited to 400W of input power.

Also, note that the maximum charge rate via DC is often much lower than the
maximum AC charge rate. *Note: In EVs, the situation is reversed.*
Additionally, note that charging circuits are often limited in how many
panels may be simultaneously connected, and many require that all panels
connected at any one time be identical. 

## Panels

From a consumer standpoint, there are two physical configurations: rigid
panels, and everything else: folding, rolling, etc. For cost per watt,
rigid panels are much less expensive. Rigid panels are usually much
better in hot and cold weather. They are also more durable. The only
advantage of folding panels is portability for camping. 

A 100W, rigid, aluminum frame panel is around $50. They are typically a
little less than 2x3 feet, and weigh about twelve pounds. A 100W folding
panel, has about the same weight as a rigid panel, is usually a little
larger than 2x3 feet when unfolded, and costs around $125.

A 100W panel is spec-ed to produce that amount of power at mid-day and mid-latitude,
under a clear sky, often at 0C. Yes, 0C. The good news is that power falls off only with the
cosine of the angle away from "directly aimed at the sun," meaning you can be 20 degrees
off and not have much loss. The bad news is that temperatures above 25C cause quite
a bit of deterioration in output. How much? I get around 78W from a 100W panel on a 
hot summer day.
